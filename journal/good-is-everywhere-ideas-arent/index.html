Good Is Everywhere Now. Ideas Aren't.

August 2026  ·  By Mark McAulay

In short: Compulsory education never raised the standard, it set a baseline and held it steady for generations. AI is what's raising that baseline now, quickly, for everyone, all at once. But the real distinction starts even before AI gets involved, with an idea that only human curiosity, lived experience and a bit of daydreaming can actually produce. The businesses that stand out are the ones supplying that spark and using the tools to build on it, not the ones waiting for the tools to supply it for them.

The baseline that never moved

Not all education is the same shape. Primary and secondary schooling was never designed to make anyone stand out. It exists to bring millions of people up to the same standard, using the same curriculum, whether they've any interest in it or not, so that a huge and varied population ends up with a shared, dependable floor to work from. That's the whole point of it, and it's a good one.

Further and higher education is a different stage entirely. Once someone's chosen a subject, there's room to breathe. Specific courses still have specific levels and outcomes to hit, but the freedom to interpret, specialise and develop an actual point of view within that structure is there. It's not the same static curriculum handed to everyone regardless of what they care about.

Even so, the standard set at the compulsory stage has remained constant underneath all of it. It's there, doing what it's always done. Useful, but it's not what makes a business or a person stand out, because everyone's been held to some version of it for years before anyone gets the chance to specialise, push back or leave the well trodden path.

Then AI arrived and raised it

AI is a different animal entirely. It doesn't hold a baseline steady, it raises it, fast.

Ask your AI tool of choice for a strategy, a headline, a first pass at anything, and it'll hand you something genuinely decent in seconds. Not decent for a beginner. Decent, full stop. That's a real shift, and I don't think it gets said often enough: the standard of "good enough to ship" has increased for almost everyone.

New tools help beginners produce solid work quickly. Fair enough, that's exactly what they're built for. But they also tempt seasoned pro's into outsourcing their original thinking for speed, because why wouldn't you take the shortcut when it's sitting right there, and it's fast, and it's genuinely good?

Beginners get better, faster than they used to. Experts get lazier, faster than they'd admit. The middle is more crowded than before, at a higher level.

There's nothing wrong with average. But who aspires to that?

There's nothing wrong with producing a literal translation of a brief if that's what's required. I've had to work to those parameters just like every other professional has, against a deadline. But commercially, it's a dangerous place to stand, and it's getting more dangerous, not less, because the standard everyone's meeting is better than it used to be. Predictable work at a higher quality is still predictable work. It's just as easily replaced.

Using modern tools and applying standard logic gets you in the room now more reliably than ever. But the room is fuller than it's ever been, and being in it isn't the same as being remembered in it.

The idea has to start somewhere else

AI can generate a genuinely impressive spread of topics on any given brief, backed by real research, connected arguments, all of it competently done. What it can't do is have the idea in the first place. Not really. Not the kind that starts a piece of work, rather than fills one out.

The spark rarely turns up while you're staring at the brief. It turns up while you're looking through old photos, listening to a record that's got nothing to do with work, or having a daft conversation with your pals about something else entirely. A thought enters sideways, right out of left field. You spot a relationship between any number of things that have no obvious business sitting next to each other, and suddenly there's a new question, or a new conclusion, about a completely different situation than the one you were thinking about five minutes ago.

That's not research. That's not pattern-matching against everything that's already been written. That's a flash of human inspiration, and it usually turns up with an emotional response attached, the feeling of having found something rather than assembled it.

AI is excellent at building the case for an idea once you've had it. It cannot go for a walk, get distracted by a song, overhear parts of conversation in a busy place and come back with something nobody asked it to think about. It doesn't get distracted at all, which is exactly the problem. Distraction, boredom, curiosity wandering off somewhere it shouldn't, that's where strong ideas often appear, and none of it happens in an AI prompt.

The human part matters most

I use Claude every day. Research, structure, a second opinion I've come to rely on. It's genuinely raised what I can produce alone, and it's done it quickly. None of that is the problem.

The point of distinction was never going to be the baseline itself, however high it climbs. It's what a person supplies before the tools even switch on, the idea, the instinct, the thing noticed sideways, and then what they apply on top of it once the tools are involved. Take that spark, take the raised baseline, take the tooling and apply real human thinking to all three, and you can move faster and go further than any of them would get you alone.

There will always be a safe middle ground, probably a more comfortable one than ever now that the tools do so much of the heavy lifting. For businesses that actually want to make a mark, safe isn't the target. They have to go further into what they're doing, and deeper, starting with an idea the tools were never going to hand them in the first place.
